## What it is

The Crewed Lunar Vehicle, or CLV-1, is an unpressurised rover that two astronauts in spacesuits can drive on the Moon. It is built by Venturi Astrolab of Hawthorne, California, with teammates Axiom Space, Interlune and Odyssey Space Research ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/2026/05/26/nasa-selects-astrolab-to-provide-lunar-rover-for-artemis-astronauts-return-to-the-moon/)). It is one of the two lunar terrain vehicles (LTVs) NASA picked for its Moon Base plan ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/suits-and-rovers/lunar-terrain-vehicle/)).

CLV-1 grew out of Astrolab's larger FLEX rover. Astrolab first said in 2023 that it would offer FLEX for the LTV competition ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/astrolab-to-send-rover-to-the-moon-on-spacexs-starship/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Terrain_Vehicle)). In April 2024 NASA chose Astrolab, Intuitive Machines and Lunar Outpost for year-long feasibility studies under the LTV Services contract, which has a combined maximum value of $4.6 billion ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-companies-to-advance-moon-mobility-for-artemis-missions/)). NASA later asked industry for smaller rovers on a faster schedule, and Astrolab adapted FLEX into CLV-1 ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/2026/05/26/nasa-selects-astrolab-to-provide-lunar-rover-for-artemis-astronauts-return-to-the-moon/)).

On 26 May 2026 NASA awarded Astrolab $219 million under a firm-fixed-price, milestone-based task order to build and deliver the rover. Over the next 18 months the companies will finish their designs, run crewed evaluations and qualify flight units. Blue Origin got $188 million, with options worth $280.4 million, to land CLV-1 and Pegasus near the south pole. NASA aims to have them there by 2028 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/)). Astrolab says CLV-1 will launch in early 2028 ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/mission-2/)).

## Why Open Moon uses it

CLV-1 is one of the two crew and cargo rovers in milestone M07 (Mobility). Suited crews can only walk a short way from a lander or habitat. A rover takes them, their tools and their samples much farther ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-companies-to-advance-moon-mobility-for-artemis-missions/)). Between crew visits it can be driven from Earth or drive itself, so it keeps scouting terrain and moving materials when no one is there ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/)).

Open Moon plans to lease rover services rather than own the hardware. That is how NASA buys them too ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-companies-to-advance-moon-mobility-for-artemis-missions/)). Flying two different rovers, CLV-1 and [Pegasus](https://www.lunaroutpost.com/pegasus), means one failure does not ground all surface travel. Without an LTV, early sorties would be limited to walking range. Site surveys for later habitat and ISRU milestones would also be slower.

## How it works

CLV-1 is a battery-electric rover. It uses the same tires, batteries and battery management system as earlier Astrolab rovers, all made by partner Venturi Space ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/2026/05/26/nasa-selects-astrolab-to-provide-lunar-rover-for-artemis-astronauts-return-to-the-moon/)). Crew sit to drive. A perception system gives semi-autonomous driving with obstacle detection and hazard avoidance. Adaptive suspension keeps the chassis level and can lower the rover to make getting on and off easier ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/clv/)). Mission Control can also run it remotely ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/clv/)).

NASA's tests of remote LTV driving put operators under 0, 4, 6 and 8 second delays. The goal was to see if a rover could cover 6 km in 24 hours. Operators drove slower and said the workload was much higher with a delay ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240001217)).

## Limitations and open issues

- **New design on a tight clock.** CLV-1 was defined after NASA changed its requirements. Flight units still have to be qualified within about 18 months ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/)).
- **No lunar flight yet.** Astrolab's heritage is from FLEX ground testing ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/2026/05/26/nasa-selects-astrolab-to-provide-lunar-rover-for-artemis-astronauts-return-to-the-moon/)). Its first Moon rover, FLIP, has not yet launched ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/event-type/launch-schedule/)).
- **Unpublished numbers.** Astrolab has not published CLV-1's battery capacity, design life, payload mass or whether it can survive the lunar night. NASA says Pegasus can run for a year but gives no lifetime for CLV-1 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/)).
- **Mostly crew, not cargo.** CLV-1 is built mainly to carry astronauts and their supplies, not external payloads ([Astrolab](https://www.astrolab.space/2026/05/26/nasa-selects-astrolab-to-provide-lunar-rover-for-artemis-astronauts-return-to-the-moon/)). Heavy hauling needs FLEX-class rovers or utility robots.
- **Depends on a lander.** Delivery relies on a Blue Origin lander and on the 2028 schedule holding ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/suits-and-rovers/lunar-terrain-vehicle/)).
- **Remote driving is slow.** Time delay cuts speed and raises operator workload ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240001217)).
