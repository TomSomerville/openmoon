## What it is

The Axiom Extravehicular Mobility Unit (AxEMU) is the spacesuit NASA's Artemis astronauts will wear to walk on the Moon. It is built by Houston company Axiom Space. It is based on NASA's own in-house Exploration EMU (xEMU) design, using NASA's technical designs and data ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axemu)). NASA spent over $420 million on the in-house xEMU before switching in April 2021 to buying spacewalks as a service ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/final-report-ig-26-006-nasas-acquisition-of-next-generation-spacesuit-services.pdf)).

Under the xEVAS contract, NASA will not own the suits. It will rent spacewalking services from the company after a first demonstration. The contracts are worth up to $3.1 billion ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/news/spacesuit-development-delays-could-impact-artemis-timeline-and-iss-operations/)). Axiom won the first task order, worth $228 million, in September 2022 to develop and demonstrate the lunar suit ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/xemu-task-award)). A prototype was shown in March 2023 ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axemu)). The white flight design, with an outer layer developed with Prada, was shown in October 2024 ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-prada-unveil-spacesuit-design-for-moon-return)).

The suit closed NASA's Critical Design Review on 13 November 2026 and now moves into production and qualification testing ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-completes-nasas-critical-design-review-for-next-generation-spacesuit)). NASA and Axiom had logged over 850 hours of crewed pressurised testing by February 2026 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/nasa-moon-mission-spacesuit-nears-milestone/)). A test on the ISS is planned for 2027, and Artemis IV in 2028 is expected to be the first mission to use it on the lunar surface ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_Extravehicular_Mobility_Unit)).

## Why Open Moon uses it

Every job outside the habitat needs a suit: setting up equipment, fixing power lines, driving rovers and collecting samples. Open Moon plans AxEMU-class suits for the crewed sorties of the first habitat (M08). Axiom is the only active provider under NASA's xEVAS spacesuit contract. Collins Aerospace, the second xEVAS supplier, left the programme in 2024, so NASA now relies on Axiom for both its lunar and ISS suits ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/news/spacesuit-development-delays-could-impact-artemis-timeline-and-iss-operations/)).

Without it, crews could not leave the cabin. The old ISS suit and the Apollo suits are not usable on the Moon. Apollo suits were custom-fitted, not built to last, and cannot be reused or refurbished ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/final-report-ig-26-006-nasas-acquisition-of-next-generation-spacesuit-services.pdf)). A suit that can be maintained and reused is also key to a base that people visit again and again.

## How it works

The suit has two main parts: a pressure garment that protects the body and lets it move, and a Portable Life Support System (PLSS) backpack. Axiom plans to finish the pressure garment and assemble the PLSS by early 2027 ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-completes-nasas-critical-design-review-for-next-generation-spacesuit)). The PLSS removes carbon dioxide with a regenerable scrubber and removes heat with a cooling system. The suit has redundant systems and onboard diagnostics ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-prada-unveil-spacesuit-design-for-moon-return)).

Next to the skin is a Liquid Cooling and Ventilation Garment. Cold water flows through tubes over the major muscles and carries body heat to the PLSS, which dumps it to space. A second, fully redundant cooling loop is a backup. A separate gas loop blows oxygen across the face to wash away exhaled carbon dioxide ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-prada-unveil-inner-layer-of-next-gen-lunar-spacesuit)). New hip and knee joints let astronauts walk and kneel instead of the "bunny hopping" seen on Apollo ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/final-report-ig-26-006-nasas-acquisition-of-next-generation-spacesuit-services.pdf)).

Axiom builds batteries and valves in-house. Partners include Prada for soft goods, Nokia for lunar communications and Oakley for the visor ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/final-report-ig-26-006-nasas-acquisition-of-next-generation-spacesuit-services.pdf)). A short-stay version, the AxEMU-S1 "Sortie suit", shares the same certified parts for early Artemis landings ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-completes-nasas-critical-design-review-for-next-generation-spacesuit)).

## Limitations and open issues

- **Schedule.** Axiom first agreed to demonstrate the suit on the Moon in November 2025. As of January 2026 it aimed for demonstration readiness in late 2027. If testing follows historical averages, demonstrations would not happen until 2031 ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/final-report-ig-26-006-nasas-acquisition-of-next-generation-spacesuit-services.pdf)).
- **Testing still ahead.** Qualification still needs performance, thermal vacuum, vibration and life-cycle testing before certification ([Axiom](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-completes-nasas-critical-design-review-for-next-generation-spacesuit)). The suit has not yet flown in space.
- **Single supplier.** With Collins gone, there is no backup suit. The OIG warned NASA could be forced to change its spacewalk plans if Axiom cannot deliver ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/news/spacesuit-development-delays-could-impact-artemis-timeline-and-iss-operations/)).
- **Contract fit.** The OIG found firm-fixed-price service contracts poorly suited to development work like spacesuits ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/news/spacesuit-development-delays-could-impact-artemis-timeline-and-iss-operations/)).
- **Dust.** On Apollo, dust caused clogging, abrasion, poor heat rejection and seal failures, and ground tests consistently underestimated it ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20050160460)). How well the AxEMU holds up over many reuses in dust is not yet shown.
- **Service cost.** The price per spacewalk for later missions is not public.
