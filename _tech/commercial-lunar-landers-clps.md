## What it is

Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) is a NASA programme that buys rides to the Moon from US companies instead of building its own landers. NASA pays a fixed price per delivery, and the company is responsible for the lander, the rocket, payload integration and surface operations ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/commercial-lunar-payload-services/)). The programme started in 2018, and the first task orders were awarded in May 2019 ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/final-report-ig-24-013-nasas-commercial-lunar-payload-services-initiative.pdf)).

NASA says it has 13 eligible companies on contract and has awarded 17 deliveries carrying more than 60 payloads. The original contracts are worth up to $4.2 billion in total through November 2028 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/commercial-lunar-payload-services/)). In November 2026 NASA picked eight companies for "CLPS 2", a 10-year contract worth up to $10 billion in total ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-awards-moon-base-transportation-technology-science-projects/)).

The flight record is mixed. Astrobotic's Peregrine (January 2024) had a valve failure that ruptured an oxidizer tank, so it never tried to land ([Astrobotic](https://www.astrobotic.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/PM1_Post-Mission-Report_Website.pdf)). Intuitive Machines' IM-1 (February 2024) made the first US soft landing in decades, but tipped over; its instruments still worked for seven days ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasa-collects-first-surface-science-in-decades-via-commercial-moon-mission/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)). Firefly's Blue Ghost Mission 1 landed upright in Mare Crisium on 2 March 2025 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/touchdown-carrying-nasa-science-fireflys-blue-ghost-lands-on-moon/)) and completed 14 days of surface operations ([Firefly](https://fireflyspace.com/news/firefly-aerospace-successfully-completes-14-days-of-surface-operations-on-the-moon)). IM-2 (March 2025) landed on its side in a crater, and its batteries ran out before the drill could fully operate ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-receives-some-data-before-intuitive-machines-ends-lunar-mission/)).

## Why Open Moon uses it

In milestone M01 (prospecting), CLPS-class landers deliver the prospecting rover, drill and instruments to the south pole. The plan calls for two prospecting landings so that one lander failure does not end the search for ice. For transport, the same landers form the "small cargo" class, under 1 t per trip, for instruments and small robots.

Buying a ride is far cheaper and faster than designing a lander. Without CLPS-class services, Open Moon would need to fund its own lander or wait for larger landers such as Blue Moon Mark 1 or Argonaut, which have not flown yet.

## How it works

NASA, or another customer, buys space on a lander. The company launches the lander on a commercial rocket, flies it to lunar orbit and lands autonomously. Each lander gives payloads power, data and heat control during flight and on the surface. Astrobotic, for example, publishes a price of $1.2 million per kg delivered to the surface ([Astrobotic](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/astrobotic-lunar-landers-pug.pdf)).

Nova-C stands 4.3 m tall and has a launch mass of about 1,900 kg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova-C)). Larger landers are joining the programme: Blue Origin's VIPER delivery is a CLPS task order worth up to $190 million ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-blue-origin-to-deliver-viper-rover-to-moons-south-pole/)).

## Limitations and open issues

- **Landing reliability.** Of the four CLPS landers flown in 2024-2025, only Blue Ghost landed upright and worked as planned ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)). Two landers tipped over and one never reached the Moon.
- **Polar landings are unproven.** The only fully successful lander landed at a mid-latitude site. IM-2, the attempt nearest the south pole, ended on its side in a crater ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-receives-some-data-before-intuitive-machines-ends-lunar-mission/)).
- **Cost growth and delays.** NASA's Inspector General found $208.2 million in cost increases and an average delay of 14 months per task order. Average time from award to launch was 44 months, against a planned 30 ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/final-report-ig-24-013-nasas-commercial-lunar-payload-services-initiative.pdf)).
- **Fragile suppliers.** The same audit notes one CLPS vendor went bankrupt and others face financial and market uncertainty ([NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/final-report-ig-24-013-nasas-commercial-lunar-payload-services-initiative.pdf)).
- **Short surface life.** Blue Ghost's payloads were planned to work for about one lunar day, about 14 Earth days ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/touchdown-carrying-nasa-science-fireflys-blue-ghost-lands-on-moon/)), and Nova-C's design life is 14 days ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova-C)). A rover must leave the lander and survive on its own.
- **Prices are not fully public.** The only per-kilogram list price we found is Astrobotic's $1.2 million per kg ([Astrobotic](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/astrobotic-lunar-landers-pug.pdf)). Other providers do not publish one.
