## What it is

The Metal 3D Printer is a technology demonstrator that made the first metal part ever printed in space. It was built for ESA by an industrial team led by Airbus Defence and Space, which also co-funded the project ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/ESA_launches_first_metal_3D_printer_to_ISS)). Airbus names AddUp, Cranfield University and Highftech Engineering as consortium partners ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2024-01-the-worlds-first-metal-3d-printer-for-space-is-on-its-way-to-the-iss)). Cranfield worked on the laser, optics and wire feeding ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Newsroom/Press_Releases/ESA_3D_prints_first_metal_part_on_the_International_Space_Station)). Earlier ISS printers only made plastic parts ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Newsroom/Press_Releases/ESA_3D_prints_first_metal_part_on_the_International_Space_Station)).

The printer launched on the Cygnus NG-20 cargo mission in January 2024 ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/ESA_launches_first_metal_3D_printer_to_ISS)). ESA astronaut Andreas Mogensen installed it in the European Drawer Rack Mark II in the [Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_(ISS_module)) module. It printed a small S-shaped test line to finish commissioning in late May 2024 ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/media/first-metal-3d-printing-on-space-station)) and completed its first full part in August 2024 ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Newsroom/Press_Releases/ESA_3D_prints_first_metal_part_on_the_International_Space_Station)). The French space agency CNES runs the prints from its CADMOS centre ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/epsilon/3D-printed_metal_unlocking_crew_autonomy)).

By July 2026 the printer had made five samples. Three had been analysed at ESTEC and DTU. ESA astronaut Sophie Adenot retrieved the fifth during the εpsilon mission ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/epsilon/3D-printed_metal_unlocking_crew_autonomy)). Trade press reports that this sample included two small thrusters designed by the German Aerospace Center (DLR), which DLR plans to hot-fire test ([VoxelMatters](https://www.voxelmatters.com/esas-iss-metal-printer-produces-further-thrusters-made-in-space/)).

## Why Open Moon uses it

Milestone M12 aims to make at least half of all spare-part mass at the base. Metal printing is one of its parts-manufacturing workstreams, building on this printer's heritage. NASA notes that about 95% of carried spares are never used, and nobody can know in advance which ones will be needed ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190030402/downloads/20190030402.pdf)). Printing parts on demand cuts that stockpile. ESA makes the same case: deep-space missions cannot rely on resupply ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Newsroom/Press_Releases/ESA_3D_prints_first_metal_part_on_the_International_Space_Station)).

It was the first metal printer to work in orbit ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Newsroom/Press_Releases/ESA_3D_prints_first_metal_part_on_the_International_Space_Station)), so it is key evidence that wire-fed laser printing works without gravity. Airbus suggests a future version could use recycled metal or processed regolith for a lunar base ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2024-01-the-worlds-first-metal-3d-printer-for-space-is-on-its-way-to-the-iss)). Without metal printing, every broken metal part, tool or bracket would have to come from Earth or be stockpiled in advance.

## How it works

The process is laser-wire directed energy deposition. A near-infrared laser melts a stainless-steel baseplate and a wire fed into the melt pool, building the part layer by layer ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/a-close-up-look-at-the-first-metal-part-made-in-space); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_energy_deposition)). The team chose wire because loose powder behaves badly in weightlessness ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2024-01-the-worlds-first-metal-3d-printer-for-space-is-on-its-way-to-the-iss)). The printer is a fully sealed box. Before printing, its air is vented to space and replaced with nitrogen so the hot steel does not oxidise ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/ESA_launches_first_metal_3D_printer_to_ISS)). Filters capture fumes ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2024-01-the-worlds-first-metal-3d-printer-for-space-is-on-its-way-to-the-iss)). The crew only opens a nitrogen and venting valve; the ground runs the print ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/media/first-metal-3d-printing-on-space-station)).

Each flight part is compared with an identical part made by the same printer on the ground before launch ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/a-close-up-look-at-the-first-metal-part-made-in-space)). An engineering model stays on Earth ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2024-01-the-worlds-first-metal-3d-printer-for-space-is-on-its-way-to-the-iss)).

## Limitations and open issues

- **No published results yet.** ESA has CT-scanned and tensile- and bend-tested returned samples, but had published no strength numbers as of its November 2025 update ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/a-close-up-look-at-the-first-metal-part-made-in-space)). In July 2026 it said results were due "in the coming weeks" ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/epsilon/3D-printed_metal_unlocking_crew_autonomy)).
- **Defects.** Microscopy of the first part showed ridges and surface flaws, and CT scans were used to map pores ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/a-close-up-look-at-the-first-metal-part-made-in-space)).
- **Anomalies.** The fifth print was stopped after an anomaly with an element only partly printed; a recovery procedure is still to be tested ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/epsilon/3D-printed_metal_unlocking_crew_autonomy)).
- **Tiny output.** Parts are small and slow: about 40 hours for a part 9 cm high ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2024-01-the-worlds-first-metal-3d-printer-for-space-is-on-its-way-to-the-iss)). A base needs far larger, faster machines.
- **One material.** Only stainless steel has flown. Printing with lunar-made metal is an idea, not a test ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2024-01-the-worlds-first-metal-3d-printer-for-space-is-on-its-way-to-the-iss)).
- **Finishing.** Parts need post-processing; ESA lists better pre- and post-processing as lessons for a future spare-parts printer ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/a-close-up-look-at-the-first-metal-part-made-in-space)).
- **Untested environment.** All printing has been in microgravity. Lunar gravity, dust and power limits have not been tested. Power draw of the printer is not published.
- **Few backups.** NASA cancelled its own metal printing project (FabLab) in May 2023 for funding reasons ([Roberts et al.](https://utw10945.utweb.utexas.edu/sites/default/files/2023/119%20NASATechnologyMaturationPlanforInSpaceManufacturingofMetals.pdf)), so few flight-proven options exist.
