## What it is

Blue Ghost is a small robotic lunar lander built and operated by Firefly Aerospace of Cedar Park, Texas. It carries small payloads to the Moon's surface and provides them with power, data and heat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firefly_Aerospace_Blue_Ghost)). NASA paid about $93.3 million in 2021 for its first flight, which carried 10 NASA science and technology payloads to Mare Crisium on the Moon's near side ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-firefly-aerospace-for-artemis-commercial-moon-delivery-in-2023/)).

Blue Ghost Mission 1 launched on a Falcon 9 on 15 January 2025 and landed on 2 March 2025 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Ghost_Mission_1)). Firefly calls it the first fully successful commercial Moon landing. The lander worked for 346 hours of daylight, then just over 5 hours into the lunar night, and sent back more than 119 GB of data, 51 GB of it science and technology data ([Firefly](https://fireflyspace.com/news/firefly-aerospace-successfully-completes-14-days-of-surface-operations-on-the-moon)). All 10 NASA payloads switched on and collected data ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-science-continues-after-fireflys-first-moon-mission-concludes/)).

More flights are booked. Mission 2 will go to the far side with ESA's Lunar Pathfinder relay satellite ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Firefly_to_take_Lunar_Pathfinder_to_the_Moon)). It has slipped into 2027, and in October 2026 the lander still had no engines installed ([The Space Review](https://www.thespacereview.com/article/5344/1)). Mission 3 targets the Gruithuisen Domes ([NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/lunar-science/clps-deliveries/cp-21-science/)). Mission 4, a $176.7 million award, targets the south pole region in 2029 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-firefly-for-new-artemis-science-tech-delivery-to-moon/)).

## Why Open Moon uses it

Open Moon's transport plan splits deliveries by size. Blue Ghost is one of three commercial landers in the "small cargo, under 1 t" class, beside Astrobotic Griffin and Intuitive Machines Nova-C. It is the only one so far with a fully successful landing that stayed upright and worked through a whole lunar day ([Firefly](https://fireflyspace.com/news/firefly-aerospace-successfully-completes-14-days-of-surface-operations-on-the-moon)). That makes it the most proven way to land instruments, small rovers and test hardware early in Phase 1.

The prospecting milestone (M01) also leans on commercial landers to deliver its instruments, and the comms milestone (M03) depends on Lunar Pathfinder, which flies on Blue Ghost Mission 2 ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Firefly_to_take_Lunar_Pathfinder_to_the_Moon)). Without Blue Ghost, Open Moon would rely on landers that have either tipped over or not yet flown, and small payloads would wait longer for a ride.

## How it works

Blue Ghost is a low, wide, four-legged lander. Its wide footprint, low centre of mass and shock-absorbing feet are meant to stop it tipping on landing ([Firefly graphic](https://fireflyspace.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/BGM1-Component-Graphic-vJan14-2025a.png)). A single main engine, the Nammo LEROS 4-ET, does the lunar orbit insertion and powered descent. Reaction control thrusters hold its attitude and help with the soft landing. Contact sensors on the legs shut the engine down at touchdown ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firefly_Aerospace_Blue_Ghost)). Vision navigation cameras guide it down without GPS ([Firefly graphic](https://fireflyspace.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/BGM1-Component-Graphic-vJan14-2025a.png)).

Solar panels charge batteries, so the lander works through one lunar day (about 14 Earth days) and dies a few hours after sunset ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-science-continues-after-fireflys-first-moon-mission-concludes/)). On Mission 2, an Elytra transfer vehicle will carry the lander and Lunar Pathfinder into lunar orbit ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Ghost_Mission_2)).

## Limitations and open issues

- **Small payload.** Up to 240 kg reaches the surface ([Firefly](https://fireflyspace.com/blue-ghost/)). Mission 1 carried only about 94 kg of NASA payloads ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-firefly-aerospace-for-artemis-commercial-moon-delivery-in-2023/)). Pilot plants, power units and big rovers need larger landers.
- **No night survival.** Mission 1 lasted one lunar day plus about 5 hours of night ([Firefly](https://fireflyspace.com/news/firefly-aerospace-successfully-completes-14-days-of-surface-operations-on-the-moon)). On Mission 2 the lander will shut down after its first day; only the LuSEE-Night payload has its own battery for the night ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Ghost_Mission_2)).
- **One success so far.** A single landing, at an easy, flat, near-side mare site, does not prove the design for polar craters or far-side terrain.
- **Schedule slips.** Mission 2 was planned for 2026 but slipped into 2027, and in October 2026 the lander still lacked its engines and propulsion parts ([The Space Review](https://www.thespacereview.com/article/5344/1)).
- **Price per kilogram is not published.** NASA prices whole task orders, not cost per kilogram, so Open Moon cannot compare cost directly with other landers.
