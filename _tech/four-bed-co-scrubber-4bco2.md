## What it is

The Four-Bed CO₂ Scrubber (called 4BCO2 or FBCO2) is a machine that pulls the carbon dioxide that people breathe out of cabin air. NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center designed, built and tested it as a redesign of the space station's original [Carbon Dioxide Removal Assembly (CDRA)](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/marshall-ships-next-generation-air-filtration-hardware-to-wallops-for-flight-to-international-space-station/), which has done this job since 2001. The CDRA had [a long history of unplanned maintenance and obsolete parts](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003542/downloads/ICES-2020-178.docx), so 4BCO2 was built to run for 3 years with no unplanned maintenance while removing the CO₂ of 4 crew at a cabin CO₂ level of 2 torr.

The unit launched to the ISS in [August 2021 and was activated in September 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240012360/downloads/ICES-2024-31.pdf), flying on [Cygnus NG-16](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/marshall-ships-next-generation-air-filtration-hardware-to-wallops-for-flight-to-international-space-station/). Its design goal was [4.16 kg of CO₂ per day at 2 mmHg](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf). It reached its goal of 3 years of run time in February 2025, became an official ISS system in July 2025, and is now the main CO₂ scrubber in the US segment. The ISS Program plans to run it full time until the station retires in 2030 ([NASA, 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf)).

In February 2025 the ISS held 12 crew during a handover and kept average CO₂ below 3.0 mmHg. In February 2026, 4BCO2 was the only CO₂ removal system for a crew of 7 ([NASA, 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf)). The site's data file lists it at TRL 7; a 2026 NASA trade study [rates it TRL 9 for lunar missions](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002853/downloads/ICES-2026-160-updated.pdf).

## Why Open Moon uses it

Every crewed habitat must remove CO₂ all the time. In Open Moon, 4BCO2 is the CO₂ removal step of life support closure in milestone [M09, permanent crew](/milestones/m09-permanent-crew/). It works next to water recovery, oxygen generation and a Sabatier reactor.

It was chosen because it is the most proven regenerative scrubber that can also feed CO₂ onward. Four-bed systems produce [high-purity, dry CO₂](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003542/downloads/ICES-2020-178.docx) that a Sabatier reactor can use without extra cleanup, which the ISS has done with CDRA ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISS_ECLSS)). In NASA's 2026 equivalent-mass study for a lunar surface habitat, 4BCO2 scored [406 kg, lower than CDRA (483 kg), the Thermal Amine Scrubber (798 kg) and the ionic-liquid system without Sabatier (419 kg)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002853/downloads/ICES-2026-160-updated.pdf). Only the Orion swing bed scored lower, and it vents much more cabin gas and cannot feed a closed loop.

Without a regenerative scrubber, the base would need single-use [lithium hydroxide canisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_scrubber) shipped from Earth for as long as crew live there, and there would be no CO₂ stream to recover water and oxygen from.

## How it works

Cabin air is drawn in by a blower and passes through two pairs of beds. In each half-cycle, a [desiccant bed takes out water vapour, then a zeolite 13X bed captures CO₂, then the air passes a second desiccant bed that puts the water back](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf) before returning to the cabin. Meanwhile the other CO₂ bed is heated by built-in heaters. An air-save pump first pulls the trapped air back for 10 minutes, then the bed is opened to space vacuum so CO₂ leaves. The beds then swap roles.

Key changes from CDRA include [round instead of rectangular beds, rod heaters, a more stable zeolite, gentler valves and a cleanable pleated filter](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003542/downloads/ICES-2020-178.docx). Filters returned in 2024 held only [0.03 g of dust, 2 to 3 orders of magnitude less than CDRA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf). A magnetic-bearing blower from Calnetix was [installed in early 2023](https://www.collectspace.com/news/news-041323a-space-station-carbon-dioxide-scrubber-upgrade.html).

## Limitations and open issues

- **Only flown in microgravity.** All flight data come from the ISS. No published test covers lunar gravity, lunar dust in the cabin, or a lunar habitat's cooling and power systems.
- **Mass of the flight unit.** The demonstration unit [far exceeded its mass target](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003542/downloads/ICES-2020-178.docx), and it [will not fit through a Gateway-sized hatch in one piece](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003542/downloads/ICES-2020-178.docx). A purpose-built lunar version still has to be designed.
- **Wearing parts.** The commercial air-save pump [failed after about 2,700 hours in 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf), and the cause is still unknown. The spare was missing a check valve, which forced a workaround until a new valve flew in 2025.
- **Power sensitivity.** A power spike tripped a breaker. The fix first limited blower speed and CO₂ removal until a [resistor bypass box was installed in 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf).
- **Shared air supply.** Removal drops when CDRA draws from the same duct, and it changes with coolant temperature ([NASA 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf)). A lunar habitat would need its own air and cooling design.
- **Vacuum and venting.** On the ISS the CO₂ is [vented to space vacuum](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf). Open Moon wants to send it to a Sabatier reactor instead. That link has worked with CDRA, but no 4BCO2 flight data with a Sabatier reactor were found.
- **Long-term zeolite life.** The CO₂ beds cannot be replaced in orbit, so NASA plans [periodic filter checks to track sorbent wear](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007665/downloads/ICES-2026-114.pdf). Life beyond about 4 years has not been shown.
