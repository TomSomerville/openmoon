## What it is

Break the Ice was a NASA Centennial Challenge that ran from 2020 to 2024. It asked the public to build robots that can dig hard, icy lunar soil (icy regolith) and haul it to a processing plant ([NTRS results paper](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)). It was led by NASA Marshall with support from Kennedy, and the total purse was $3.5 million ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/prizes-challenges-crowdsourcing-program/centennial-challenges/six-finalists-named-in-nasas-3-5-million-break-the-ice-challenge/)).

Phase 1 was paper designs: 31 teams submitted and 13 shared $500,000 ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)). In Phase 2, teams built real robots and ran them for 15 days at their own sites, then six finalists competed in Huntsville, Alabama, in June 2024. Terra Engineering won $1 million with its "Fracture" rover and Starpath Robotics came second ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/marshall/california-teams-win-1-5-million-in-nasas-break-the-ice-lunar-challenge)). Starpath won $500,000 at the finals ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)).

The prize included a test in a NASA thermal vacuum chamber. Starpath spent about three days in Marshall's 20-foot V-20 chamber in August 2025, digging and driving on a concrete slab with rocky terrain ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/prizes-challenges-crowdsourcing-program/centennial-challenges/lunar-challenge-winner-tests-technology-in-nasa-thermal-vacuum-chamber/), [NASA images](https://images.nasa.gov/details/MSFC-STARPATH-07-30-2025-joek-30)). NASA has not published numerical results from that test. None of these robots has flown.

## Why Open Moon uses it

Water ice at the poles is the cheapest source of water, oxygen and propellant on the Moon. It is thought to sit in permanently shadowed craters, mixed into the soil as small grains or coatings rather than as solid sheets ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_water)). To use it, a base must first dig it up and move it.

In Open Moon, this technology does that job in **M06 (ISRU pilot)**. It feeds icy regolith to the water extraction and electrolysis plant. Without it, M06 can only make oxygen from dry regolith, and water for life support and propellant must still come from Earth. NASA lists hard icy material as a technology gap. Its target is 1,000 t of icy regolith to make 50 t of water ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)). The challenge robots are far smaller than that, but they are the best tested designs for this job.

## How it works

The rules modelled a polar crater. In Phase 2, the "ice" was a weak concrete with a compressive strength of 1.5–2.0 MPa. That approximates icy regolith with 4% water ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957), [rules](https://breaktheicechallenge.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/FINAL-BTIL-Ph2-rules.pdf)). Each robot had to dig and deliver 800 kg a day for 15 days (12 t in total), driving 500 m each way per load, on its own batteries and radios ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)).

The winners took different approaches. Starpath uses a pair of opposing bucket drums, like NASA's RASSOR and IPEx, with a lifting hopper that dumps like a dump truck ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)). Opposing drums cancel out digging forces, so a light robot can dig in low gravity ([IPEx](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240008162)). Terra Engineering uses many small hardened milling bits that scrape and then crack the surface upward ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)).

At the finals, a crane held up 5/6 of each robot's weight during digging to mimic lunar gravity. Scores favoured a high dig rate for the robot's mass and for the energy used ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957), [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QAgOCjbvODs)).

|---|---|---|
| Robot mass | 325.3 kg | 253.8 kg |
| Dug and collected | 311.6 kg in 60:00 | 91.5 kg in 32:20 |
| Digging energy | 250 Wh | 219 Wh |

## Limitations and open issues

- **Not real ice.** The test material was weakened concrete, not icy lunar soil. Real ice at below about 100 K may behave differently ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_water)).
- **Not built for space.** Phase 2 did not require space-rated parts, lunar thermal protection or lighting ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)).
- **Endurance unproven.** Only 2 of 8 teams met 800 kg a day for all 15 days ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)). A real base needs years of operation.
- **Gravity only partly simulated.** Gravity offload was used for digging but not for hauling ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240015957)).
- **Vacuum results unpublished.** NASA has not released numbers from the thermal vacuum test ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/prizes-challenges-crowdsourcing-program/centennial-challenges/lunar-challenge-winner-tests-technology-in-nasa-thermal-vacuum-chamber/)).
- **No flight.** No flight date has been announced for either winner. The closest flight test, PRIME-1, got about 10 hours of its planned 10 days of work after the IM-2 lander tipped over ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasas-lunar-drill-technology-passes-tests-on-the-moon/)).
- **Ice form uncertain.** Nobody yet knows how much ice there is or how it is spread through the soil, so dig targets may change ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_water)).
