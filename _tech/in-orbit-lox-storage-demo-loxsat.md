## What it is
LOXSAT (the Liquid Oxygen Flight Demonstration) is a small satellite that will store and move liquid oxygen in low Earth orbit. It tests the hardware a future [propellant depot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant_depot) needs. Over a [nine-month mission it will demonstrate 11 cryogenic fluid management technologies](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/tech-demo-missions-program/cryogenic-fluid-management-cfm/nasa-industry-prepare-cryogenic-fuel-technology-demo/), covering boiloff, transfer, tank pressure and gauging how much liquid is left.

The payload is built by Eta Space, a small Florida company. NASA picked it in its October 2020 Tipping Point round with an [award of about $27 million](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-announces-partners-to-advance-tipping-point-technologies-for-the-moon-mars/). Rocket Lab supplies the [Photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab_Photon) spacecraft bus and the Electron launch. In October 2025 Rocket Lab said it had [finished the spacecraft](https://investors.rocketlabcorp.com/news-releases/news-release-details/rocket-lab-completes-spacecraft-cryogenic-fueling-mission-eta). Engineers from NASA's [Cryogenic Fluid Management project](https://www.nasa.gov/space-technology-mission-directorate/tdm/cryogenic-fluid-management-cfm/) at Marshall, Glenn and Kennedy support the mission.

In May 2026 NASA said the payload was integrated and would launch from New Zealand [no earlier than 17 July 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/tech-demo-missions-program/cryogenic-fluid-management-cfm/nasa-industry-prepare-cryogenic-fuel-technology-demo/). That date has passed. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LOXSAT) now calls it the "originally scheduled" date and still lists LOXSAT as a future flight. Eta Space plans to use the results for a commercial depot called Cryo-Dock, [planned for about 2030](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LOXSAT).

## Why Open Moon uses it
Open Moon does not fly LOXSAT. It uses LOXSAT as **heritage**: flight data that shows liquid oxygen can be kept cold and moved in space without big losses. Milestone M11 (Propellant and industrial-scale ISRU) needs a cryogenic depot at the landing pads, with liquefaction, storage tanks, transfer lines and boil-off control. Landers there would be refuelled with lunar oxygen.

Keeping oxygen liquid is hard. Heat leaks in, the liquid boils, and pressure rises. [Insulation alone cannot stop the loss](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/cryogenicfluidmanagement-factsheet.pdf); a cryocooler that removes the heat can bring it close to zero, which NASA calls a zero-boiloff system. LOXSAT is one of the first flights built to test this with oxygen. Without such data, M11 would have to size its tanks and coolers from ground tests and models alone, or plan for extra oxygen to make up for boiloff.

## How it works
The spacecraft carries a spherical liquid oxygen tank on a Photon bus. Its active thermal control system [pulls liquid from the tank through a propellant management device, pumps it through a cryocooler, and sprays the chilled liquid back into the gas space](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220018686). The cold droplets cool the gas and condense vapour, so pressure drops. Liquid that is not sprayed returns colder and cools the bulk. The [cryocooler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryocooler) works much like a refrigerator.

NASA and Eta Space have modelled this because spray heat transfer in zero gravity [has little test data](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220018686). Their two models agree in broad terms, but one predicts more subcooled liquid and the other a better-mixed tank near boiling point.

## Limitations and open issues
- **Not flown yet.** The NASA date of 17 July 2026 [has slipped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LOXSAT), and no new date is confirmed in the sources here.
- **Small scale.** LOXSAT is a small satellite. M11 needs storage of hundreds of tonnes; scaling up is unproven.
- **Orbit, not the Moon.** It tests microgravity. A lunar depot works in one-sixth gravity, with dust and big day-night temperature swings.
- **Uncertain physics.** Zero-g spray cooling [is not well characterised](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220018686); flight data is needed to anchor the models.
- **Cryocooler reliability.** The last flown zero-boiloff test, RRM3 on the ISS, [lost its methane after a cryocooler failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotic_Refueling_Mission) about four months in.
- **Key numbers missing.** Tank size, cooling power and boiloff targets are not published in the sources here.
