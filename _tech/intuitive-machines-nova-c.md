## What it is

Nova-C is a small robotic Moon lander built by Intuitive Machines of Houston, Texas. It is a hexagonal craft about 4.3 m tall on six legs. It weighs about 1,900 kg at launch and 675 kg dry, and it is quoted as carrying 100 kg to over 130 kg of payload ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines_Nova-C)). Its design grew out of NASA's Morpheus test lander, which several of the company's founders worked on ([NASA Spinoff](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/node/11441); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Morpheus)).

NASA picked Nova-C in May 2019 as one of the first landers for its Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) programme. The first award was $77 million and grew to $118 million by January 2024 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)). The first mission, IM-1 *Odysseus*, touched down near Malapert A in the south polar region on 22 February 2024 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-1)). It was the first commercial Moon landing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)). It broke a leg on touchdown and came to rest tilted at about 30 degrees, but its six NASA instruments worked for seven days and sent back more than 500 MB of data ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasa-collects-first-surface-science-in-decades-via-commercial-moon-mission/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines_Nova-C)).

The second mission, IM-2 *Athena*, landed on Mons Mouton near the south pole on 6 March 2025. It ended up on its side in a cold, shadowed crater, and its batteries ran down the next day ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-receives-some-data-before-intuitive-machines-ends-lunar-mission/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-2)). The third mission, IM-3, heads for the Reiner Gamma swirl. NASA now lists it for early 2027 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/event/clps-flight-intuitive-machines-im-3/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-3)).

## Why Open Moon uses it

Open Moon's transport milestone (M02) buys small cargo rides of under 1 t from commercial landers instead of building its own. Nova-C is one of three named options, next to Firefly Blue Ghost and Astrobotic Griffin. The plan wants two providers per payload where possible. Nova-C gives that second (or third) supplier for instruments, small robots and early site surveys.

Nova-C also has the most flight experience of any US commercial lander: two landings near the south pole, the region Open Moon targets ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines_Nova-C)). Both missions returned data, but neither landed upright. Open Moon therefore treats Nova-C as a useful but not yet proven carrier. Without it, small-cargo delivery would rest on one flown lander (Blue Ghost) and one untested lander (Griffin).

## How it works

Nova-C launches on a SpaceX Falcon 9 and flies itself to the Moon. Its main engine, the VR900, burns liquid methane and liquid oxygen. Helium gas pushes the propellants into the engine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines_Nova-C)). This is the same propellant pair NASA tested on Morpheus. Both fuels could in principle be made from local resources one day ([NASA Spinoff](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/node/11441)). Small helium cold-gas thrusters steer the craft. Solar panels and a battery provide power.

For landing, the lander uses laser rangefinders and cameras that match craters against Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter maps. It then descends under engine power onto its six legs ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/im-2s-imperfect-landing-due-to-altimeter-interference-south-pole-lighting-conditions/)).

## Limitations and open issues

- **No upright landing yet.** Both missions ended tilted or on their side, which cut surface operations to days (IM-1) or hours (IM-2) ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasa-collects-first-surface-science-in-decades-via-commercial-moon-mission/); [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-receives-some-data-before-intuitive-machines-ends-lunar-mission/)).
- **Landing sensors are the weak point.** On IM-1, a safety switch on the primary laser rangefinder was left inactive before launch. Controllers switched to a NASA Navigation Doppler Lidar payload instead ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-1)). On IM-2, noise disturbed the laser altimeter, and long polar shadows confused crater-matching navigation ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/im-2s-imperfect-landing-due-to-altimeter-interference-south-pole-lighting-conditions/)).
- **Fixes not yet flown.** For IM-3 the company plans redundant altimeters of different types, a velocity sensor that works in any lighting, and a larger crater database ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/im-2s-imperfect-landing-due-to-altimeter-interference-south-pole-lighting-conditions/)). IM-3 goes to Reiner Gamma, not the south pole ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IM-3)). So it will not prove landings in polar lighting.
- **Tall and narrow.** A 4.3 m height on a 1.6 m-wide body makes tipping more likely on rough ground. The lander is only designed for slopes of up to 10° ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines_Nova-C)).
- **Short surface life.** Nova-C has no published way to survive the lunar night. IM-1 did not wake after its first night ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines_Nova-C)).
- **Schedule slips.** IM-3 was first planned for 2024 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services)) and is now listed for early 2027 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/event/clps-flight-intuitive-machines-im-3/)).
- **Small payload.** About 100-130 kg is enough for instruments, not for rovers or power plants. Larger cargo needs Nova-D or other landers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive_Machines_Nova-C)).
