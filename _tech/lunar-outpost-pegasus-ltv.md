## What it is

Pegasus is an open, two-seat electric rover for astronauts at the Moon's south pole. It is built by Lunar Outpost, a company in Golden, Colorado. NASA calls it [a lighter, mission-ready evolution of the company's Eagle rover](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/), designed for NASA's updated crewed lunar terrain vehicle (LTV) requirements. Lunar Outpost leads the team, with [General Motors, Goodyear and Leidos](https://www.lunaroutpost.com/post/nasa-selects-lunar-outpost-to-deliver-next-gen-crewed-lunar-terrain-vehicle-for-artemis-astronauts-a) as partners.

NASA started its LTV Services contract in April 2024. It picked three companies for year-long feasibility studies, and the contract has a [combined maximum value of $4.6 billion](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-companies-to-advance-moon-mobility-for-artemis-missions/). NASA does not buy the rover. It buys a service. In May 2026 NASA gave [Lunar Outpost $220 million](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/) to build and deliver Pegasus, and Astrolab $219 million for its CLV-1. The goal is to have both on the surface by 2028. Blue Origin was awarded $188 million to land them.

By October 2026, Lunar Outpost had built [two Pegasus mockups and delivered a third](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/moon-base/nasa-provides-progress-update-on-moon-base-rovers/) to NASA Johnson. Astronauts in pressurised suits tested it there, and [NASA photos](https://images.nasa.gov/details/jsc2026e417620) show tests in September 2026. The preliminary design review was set for October 2026. Flight hardware is due to NASA at the end of 2027. The company says [November 2027](https://www.lunaroutpost.com/pegasus).

## Why Open Moon uses it

Pegasus is one of the two LTV-class rovers in milestone M07 (Mobility). Open Moon does not build crew rovers. It plans to lease LTV services. Crews use the rovers to reach work and science sites. Between crew visits, people on Earth drive them to map terrain and haul loads for the road and landing pad work in M05 and M06.

M07 is complete when both LTVs have each driven at least 100 km, including driverless trips commanded from Earth. With only one LTV, the base has no backup if that rover breaks down. With no LTV, crews could only work within walking range of the lander and habitat. NASA's [LTV page](https://www.nasa.gov/moonbase/lunar-terrain-vehicle/) gives the same reason: crews can go farther and do more science than on foot.

## How it works

Pegasus is a low, open rover in the style of the Apollo [Lunar Roving Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Roving_Vehicle). It carries [two astronauts side by side](https://www.lunaroutpost.com/post/nasa-selects-lunar-outpost-to-deliver-next-gen-crewed-lunar-terrain-vehicle-for-artemis-astronauts-a) and is designed so they can get in and out quickly. GM is adapting its electric vehicle technology into [space-rated batteries](https://www.lunaroutpost.com/post/nasa-selects-lunar-outpost-to-deliver-next-gen-crewed-lunar-terrain-vehicle-for-artemis-astronauts-a). Pegasus has [spring mesh tyres](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/moon-base/nasa-provides-progress-update-on-moon-base-rovers/), which are being tested at NASA Glenn. A thermal control system keeps it working through the large temperature swings at the pole.

It can be driven three ways: by crew on board, by remote control from Earth, or on its own. Its thermal control, navigation cameras and mission control software were [flight-proven on Lunar Outpost's MAPP rover](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/moon-base/nasa-provides-progress-update-on-moon-base-rovers/) at the south pole in 2025. NASA tests showed that people on Earth could [drive a reference LTV with 4, 6 and 8 second delays](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240001217) through every test terrain. They drove more slowly and found the work harder.

## Limitations and open issues

- **No crewed rover of this type has flown yet.** Pegasus is still a mockup and prototype. Its preliminary design review was due in [October 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/moon-base/nasa-provides-progress-update-on-moon-base-rovers/), and drivable mockups have not yet been tested with crew.
- **Its ride to the Moon has not flown.** Blue Origin's first Blue Moon Mk1 lander is now listed for launch [no earlier than Q1 2027](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Moon_Pathfinder_Mission_1). A delay there pushes back the 2028 rover delivery.
- **Tight schedule.** NASA gave the companies about [18 months](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-moon-base-rovers-landers-missions/) to finish the design, test with crew and qualify flight units.
- **Cold and dark.** The rovers will drive in and out of permanently shadowed regions that can reach [-203°C](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/moon-base/nasa-provides-progress-update-on-moon-base-rovers/). How well they save energy there is still one of the things NASA wants to learn.
- **Remote driving is slow and tiring.** In NASA tests, drivers on Earth [braked more and reported higher workload](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240001217) when there was a time delay.
- **Missing numbers.** Lunar Outpost has not published Pegasus's mass, battery capacity, payload limit or tyre life.
- **One-year life.** The design life is [up to a year](https://www.lunaroutpost.com/pegasus). A base that runs for many years will need replacements or a longer-lived successor.
