## What it is

The Additive Manufacturing Facility (AMF) is a plastic 3D printer that lives permanently on the International Space Station. It was built by Made In Space, Inc. under a NASA small business (SBIR) contract and installed in April 2016 ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190033503/downloads/20190033503.pdf)). It is the company's second-generation printer. The first, a NASA technology demonstrator that flew in 2014, made 55 ABS parts in orbit ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190030402/downloads/20190030402.pdf)). Redwire acquired Made In Space in June 2020 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Made_In_Space)), so AMF is now a Redwire product.

Unlike the demonstrator, AMF is owned by the company and sold as a service ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/33166-space-station-commercial-3d-printer-first-tool-photos.html)). Customers reach it through the ISS National Laboratory, and NASA buys prints through an IDIQ contract ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190033503/downloads/20190033503.pdf)). Its first commercial part was a Kobalt wrench in June 2016. It printed a finger splint in January 2017, the first medical item made in orbit ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/newsroom/2019-3-28-additive-manufacturing-facility-3d-printing-the-future-in-space/)). By March 2018 it had made nearly 100 parts and was printing for customers almost every week ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/newsroom/made-in-space-celebrates-two-years-printing-in-space-with-amf/)).

NASA parts include a tow hitch for the SPHERES free-flying satellites (printed 21 Feb 2017), an enclosure for radiation monitors in the BEAM module, an antenna feed horn and an air-flow probe adapter ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190030367/downloads/20190030367.pdf)). In 2022 it printed an insert for StemRad's AstroRad radiation vest from low-density polyethylene ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/newsroom/redwire-team-completes-successful-3d-print-for-radiation-shielding-on-orbit/)). Redwire still lists AMF as an operating platform ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/capabilities/research-and-manufacturing/)). We found no dated public statement of its status in 2025 or 2026.

## Why Open Moon uses it

Open Moon's manufacturing milestone (M12) aims to "print what breaks", using polymer printing and recycling alongside metal printing. AMF is the heritage for the polymer side. It is the longest-running commercial printer in space, and it has made real station hardware rather than only test coupons.

The case is about logistics. NASA says the current approach of launching spares will not work for deep space missions. It says in-space manufacturing could greatly cut the mass of maintenance spares, especially with recycling and local resources ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190030367/downloads/20190030367.pdf)). AMF has also printed with lunar regolith simulant through the Redwire Regolith Print payload, the first time regolith simulant was used for 3D printing in space ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/newsroom/redwire-regolith-manufacturing-technology-wins-popular-science-2021-best-of-whats-new-award/)). Without a printer like this, every broken plastic part on the base would need a spare carried from Earth.

## How it works

AMF uses fused filament fabrication. A heated nozzle melts a plastic filament and lays it down layer by layer on a moving bed ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190030402/downloads/20190030402.pdf)). It sits in an EXPRESS rack on the station. For hard materials like LDPE, Redwire fitted a custom extruder and print bed ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/newsroom/redwire-team-completes-successful-3d-print-for-radiation-shielding-on-orbit/)). The hardware is modular, so it can be upgraded in orbit ([Redwire](https://rdw.com/newsroom/made-in-space-celebrates-two-years-printing-in-space-with-amf/)). Part files are sent up from the ground and printed in hours, instead of waiting for a cargo flight.

## Limitations and open issues

- **Plastic only.** AMF prints thermoplastics. Load-bearing or hot parts need metal printing, which is only at demonstration level ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Newsroom/Press_Releases/ESA_3D_prints_first_metal_part_on_the_International_Space_Station)).
- **Small parts.** The 10 x 10 x 14 cm build volume limits it to small tools, brackets and covers.
- **Feedstock comes from Earth.** AMF does not recycle by itself. Closing the loop needs a separate recycler, such as the Refabricator demonstration ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/refabricator-recycle-reuse-plastic-installed-space-station/)).
- **Regolith printing is early.** The regolith simulant prints proved the process steps, not useful structural parts.
- **Material qualification.** NASA was still running materials studies of AMF-printed ABS in 2019 ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190030402/downloads/20190030402.pdf)). Flight-critical use needs certified properties.
- **Gaps in public data.** Mass, power use, total parts printed after 2018 and current operating status are not published.
- **Never run on the Moon.** AMF works inside a pressurised module in microgravity. Lunar gravity, dust and a surface habitat's power budget are untested.
