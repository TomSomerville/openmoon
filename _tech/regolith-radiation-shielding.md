## What it is

Regolith radiation shielding means piling the Moon's own loose soil over a habitat so that the soil absorbs incoming radiation. [Regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_regolith) is the layer of crushed rock and dust that covers the lunar surface. It is already there, so it does not have to be launched from Earth. That is the main appeal.

The idea is old. A 1988 study for the Universities Space Research Association compared [eight ways to shield a lunar base with soil](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19890006903) and chose a module partly buried in the ground. Later NASA concepts include [ARMOR](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20130009378), an arch-shaped jacket filled with a 3 m shell of unprocessed regolith. In 2013 [ESA and Foster + Partners](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Engineering_Technology/Building_a_lunar_base_with_3D_printing) studied 3D-printing regolith walls over an inflatable dome and printed a 1.5 tonne demonstration block from simulant.

No regolith shield has been built on the Moon. Work so far is modelling and lab testing. There is [no single NASA design value](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006713/downloads/nasem_presentation%20v07012025%20vf.pdf) for how thick it should be, and NASA says sizing shields thicker than 100 g/cm² is not yet possible because of large physics uncertainty.

## Why Open Moon uses it

The lunar surface is harsh. China's Chang'e-4 lander measured an average dose equivalent of [1,369 µSv per day](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaz1334). NASA's career limit is [600 mSv](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf) for every astronaut. Without shielding, a year on the surface would use most of a career.

Milestone M09 (permanent crew presence) depends on this. Four crew live on the surface in six-month rotations. Shipping that much shielding mass from Earth would be very costly. A [2024 IAC study](https://iafastro.directory/iac/paper/id/82864/abstract-pdf/IAC-24,D3,IP,1,x82864.brief.pdf) found regolith-only shielding cost competitive, because launch mass drives settlement cost.

The same study, using NASA's OLTARIS tool, found about 150 mSv per 180-day mission behind 2 m of regolith and about 100 mSv behind 3 m. Open Moon therefore sets 2 m as a minimum and 3 m as a target. Without it, crews would need short stays or shielding shipped from Earth.

## How it works

Galactic cosmic rays are fast, heavy charged particles. When they hit matter, they lose energy and break apart into lighter particles. [Regolith shields about as well as aluminium](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006713/downloads/nasem_presentation%20v07012025%20vf.pdf) per unit of mass. Solar particle events are mostly lower-energy protons, which are [shielded much more easily](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210009760/downloads/Clowdsley_Lunar_Radiation_Talk_v2.pdf).

Thin shields can make things worse. Fragments from the [shield itself](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20030060559) can be more penetrating, so "some shielding is worse than none at all." The IAC study found dose actually rises up to about 1 m of regolith, then falls. That is why the design uses metres, not centimetres. NASA advises not adding mass beyond 20 g/cm² [unless the concept adds metres of shielding](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210009760/downloads/Clowdsley_Lunar_Radiation_Talk_v2.pdf), such as a buried habitat.

Designers check a layout with [OLTARIS](https://oltaris.nasa.gov), NASA's web tool that combines environment models, particle transport and human body models with a ray-trace of the habitat.

## Limitations and open issues

- **Thick-shield physics is uncertain.** NASA calls for [dose-depth measurements beyond 100 g/cm²](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006713/downloads/nasem_presentation%20v07012025%20vf.pdf) and proposes stacking regolith over detectors on the Moon to measure it.
- **One study sets the numbers.** The 2 m and 3 m figures come from a [student conference abstract](https://iafastro.directory/iac/paper/id/82864/abstract-pdf/IAC-24,D3,IP,1,x82864.brief.pdf). They exclude outdoor work and other shielding materials.
- **No design requirement yet.** NASA has [not released radiation design requirements](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210009760/downloads/Clowdsley_Lunar_Radiation_Talk_v2.pdf) for lunar surface habitats.
- **Construction is unproven.** Bagging, piling or printing metres of regolith around a module has only been done as concepts and Earth demos, such as [ESA's printed block](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Engineering_Technology/Building_a_lunar_base_with_3D_printing). ESA also flagged dust control as needing more study.
- **Solar storms still need a plan.** NASA suggests a [storm shelter or wearable protection](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210009760/downloads/Clowdsley_Lunar_Radiation_Talk_v2.pdf) for large solar particle events, and dose outside the berm during surface work is not covered by the burial.
- **It traps heat.** Regolith conducts heat very poorly: about [0.009–0.013 W/m·K](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/5787569) in bulk from Apollo, and as low as [0.00074 W/m·K](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.00977) in the loose surface layer from Diviner. A buried habitat sits in steady surroundings, but its own heat cannot escape through the cover, so it needs pumped loops to radiators on the surface. NASA advises against burying power cables [for the same reason](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250000841).
- **Excavation mass and time.** The tonnes moved and machine hours needed for a full burial are not published for a specific design.
