## What it is

This is a family of landing-pad surfaces made mostly from local rock dust. One version uses **sintered basalt pavers**: tiles made by heating basalt grains until they fuse, without fully melting them ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintering)). The other uses **regolith-polymer pads**: regolith bound with a silicone rubber (RTV) that cures at room temperature. The two can be combined, with polymer grout filling the seams between sintered pavers ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)).

The paver idea was first tried at full scale in Hawaii. The Pacific International Space Center for Exploration Systems (PISCES) and NASA Kennedy's Swamp Works planned a 9 m² pad of basalt pavers in 2015 ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2015/06/01/pisces-launches-robotic-space-construction-project/)). The Helelani rover placed the pavers; about 100 were needed ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2015/12/17/pisces-scientists-make-headway-at-lunar-landing-pad-site/)). In March 2016 a four-second rocket burn on the pad displaced some of the interlocking pavers ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2016/03/21/pisces-rocket-engine-firing-test-mission-complete/)).

In 2020-2021 NASA Kennedy's "LPAD" project, a partnership with SpaceX, hot-fire tested pad materials with Masten Space Systems in the Mojave Desert ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/kennedy/nasa-tests-landing-pad-materials-for-future-lunar-missions)). Fifteen tests were run. Grouted sintered basalt pavers and polymer-regolith materials were among the most promising ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210020323)). Nothing has yet been built or tested on the Moon.

## Why Open Moon uses it

Rocket exhaust on bare regolith throws particles at over 2,000 m/s for kilometres ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20205007522)). Apollo 12 sandblasted the Surveyor 3 probe from about 180 m away ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Engineering_Technology/How_to_make_roads_on_the_Moon)). Artemis-class landers have more thrust, so the risk to nearby equipment is larger ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)).

Milestone M05 (site preparation) needs at least two hardened pads before regular cargo and crew landings. Sintered pavers are the main option there; a regolith-polymer pad is the fallback. Without a pad, every landing would sandblast the solar towers, reactor and habitats, and the base would have to spread out much further.

## How it works

**Sintered pavers.** Basalt fines are heated in moulds into tiles. PISCES reports sintered basalt is stronger than commercial concrete, but the process needs a lot of power ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2021/12/13/basalt-binder-research/)). Tiles interlock, and the seams can be grouted. A PISCES tile held up well against a Masten plume at about 25% of the Apollo landing heat flux ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2020/06/02/preparing-for-a-long-term-presence-on-the-moon/)).

**Regolith-polymer pads.** Regolith simulant is mixed with RTV-655 silicone. No bulk heating is needed and mineralogy matters little. A twin-screw extruder mixed and laid the material at up to 90% regolith by mass ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)).

In the 2021 dynamic tests, the polymer pavers and the grouted sintered pavers showed heat scarring but little to no erosion. All test articles stopped regolith ejecta ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)).

## Limitations and open issues

- **Low readiness.** In 2022 the KSC team put pad construction as a whole at about TRL 2-3. It recommended work towards TRL 6 for a small lunar demo ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)).
- **Small engines only.** Tests used a 444 N engine in Earth's atmosphere. NASA notes this plume is not representative of lunar vacuum ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210012836)).
- **Imported binder mass.** RTV cannot be made on the Moon. A 200 m pad would need 282 t, more than one Starship load ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)).
- **Lunar environment untested.** Thermal cycling, radiation and vacuum effects on the polymer have not been measured ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)).
- **Erosion and displacement.** A 20% RTV paver eroded a 3.07 cm by 3.2 cm cavity under a close static plume ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220000334)). Pavers moved in the 2016 Hawaii test ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2016/03/21/pisces-rocket-engine-firing-test-mission-complete/)).
- **Sintering power.** Sintering takes a lot of energy, and scaling tiles up is hard ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2021/12/13/basalt-binder-research/)). No published figure gives energy per square metre of lunar pad.
- **Robotic laying.** Pavers have only been laid by a rover on Earth ([PISCES](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2015/12/17/pisces-scientists-make-headway-at-lunar-landing-pad-site/)). Autonomous construction on the Moon is unproven.
