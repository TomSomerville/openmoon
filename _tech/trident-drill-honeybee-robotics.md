## What it is

TRIDENT (The Regolith and Ice Drill for Exploring New Terrain) is a 1 m rotary-percussive drill built by [Honeybee Robotics](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/rover-and-instruments) of Altadena, California. It spins to cut and hammers to break hard material. Its job is to bring soil from below the lunar surface up to where other instruments can sniff it for water and other volatiles ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasas-lunar-drill-technology-passes-tests-on-the-moon/)). Honeybee is now a [subsidiary of Blue Origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeybee_Robotics).

The design comes from [two decades of Honeybee drill work](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51). Earlier versions included the Icebreaker drill (over 50 kg) and the lighter LITA drill (15 kg), which was tested on a rover in the Atacama Desert. Two flight units were built: one for NASA's PRIME-1 lander payload and one for the VIPER rover ([Zacny et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51)).

PRIME-1 flew on Intuitive Machines' IM-2 lander, Athena, which launched in February 2025 and landed on 6 March about 400 m from its target near Mons Mouton. The lander came to rest on its side in a crater, and the mission lasted about 10 hours instead of 10 days ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasas-lunar-drill-technology-passes-tests-on-the-moon/)). Even so, TRIDENT powered up, rotated, hammered, cycled its heater and extended to its full range. Because the lander was on its side, the drill deployed horizontally and [never penetrated regolith](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51). The VIPER unit was [installed in the rover in February 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/general/into-the-belly-of-the-rover-vipers-final-science-instrument-installed). After VIPER's cancellation and revival, NASA picked Blue Origin to deliver it [in late 2027 on a Blue Moon MK1 lander](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-blue-origin-to-deliver-viper-rover-to-moons-south-pole/).

## Why Open Moon uses it

Milestone M01 (prospecting) needs ice measured by drilling, not only from orbit. Its exit criteria call for holes at least 1 m deep at several points in at least two permanently shadowed regions. The planned prospector rovers carry a "TRIDENT-heritage" 1 m drill alongside an MSolo-class mass spectrometer. That pairing is exactly what PRIME-1 flew and what VIPER will carry ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasas-lunar-drill-technology-passes-tests-on-the-moon/)).

TRIDENT is the closest thing to a flight-qualified, rover-ready US ice drill. It has worked on the Moon, if only in a partial test, and a second unit is [fully integrated in a qualified rover](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51). Its drilling telemetry also gives regolith strength, which M01 needs for siting landing pads and habitats ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/general/into-the-belly-of-the-rover-vipers-final-science-instrument-installed)). Without it, Open Moon would have to wait for ESA's or JAXA's drills (see alternatives) or fund a new drill. The water and propellant plants in later milestones would then be sized on less certain ice numbers.

## How it works

TRIDENT has [six subsystems](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51): a drill head with separate rotary and percussive motors; a deployment stage that lowers the drill and presses it to the ground; a feed stage that drives the drill string up to 1 m down; the drill string, with a carbide bit, two temperature sensors and a heater; a brushing station; and an avionics box.

It samples in [10 cm "bites"](https://lsic.jhuapl.edu/uploadedDocs/posters/401-Poster%20PDF_8-Chu.pdf). The drill cuts 10 cm, the spiral flutes hold the cuttings, the drill pulls up, and a rotating brush sweeps them into a [neat pile on the ground](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/rover-and-instruments). Instruments then analyse the pile. Repeating this gives a rough depth profile. The bit sensor measures subsurface temperature, and the heater, 35 cm above the bit, supports thermal conductivity measurements and can free a drill that freezes in ([Zacny et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51)). Each hammer blow also makes a seismic signal that a lander or rover motion sensor can pick up.

## Limitations and open issues

- **No hole drilled on the Moon yet.** On PRIME-1 all mechanisms worked, but the drill [did not penetrate regolith](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51). Drilling into polar ice is still unproven in flight.
- **Lander dependence.** PRIME-1 was lost to a tipped lander, and VIPER's ride is [still in the future](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-blue-origin-to-deliver-viper-rover-to-moons-south-pole/). Open Moon plans two landers for this reason.
- **Binding in cemented soil.** Ground tests in cemented lunar simulant saw [cuttings build up and the drill choke and bind](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240000585). Field tests at Haughton Crater, the Bishop Tuff and [Antarctica](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20260007172) are working on fault detection.
- **Cold testing limits.** Vacuum tests ran below −100 °C with simulant holding up to 8.5 wt% water ([Zacny et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ae0b51)). Performance at the much colder floors of shadowed craters, and in real lunar ice, is not published.
- **Ice loss and contamination.** Cuttings sit on the surface before analysis, so some ice may sublimate. On PRIME-1, the gases MSolo saw were [from spacecraft propellant and traces of Earth water](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/clps/nasas-lunar-drill-technology-passes-tests-on-the-moon/), and a [follow-up NASA review](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260003150/downloads/MSolo%20PRIME-1%20overview%20and%20results%20-%20LSSW.pdf) covers those results.
- **Cost not published.** No unit price for TRIDENT is public.
