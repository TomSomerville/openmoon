## What it is

Vertical Solar Array Technology (VSAT) is a NASA project to build solar arrays that stand upright on tall masts on the Moon. The arrays deploy on their own, reach up to about 32 feet (about 10 m) high, and can retract so they can be moved ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/three-companies-to-help-nasa-advance-solar-array-technology-for-moon/)). The project is run by NASA's Game Changing Development program and Langley Research Center, with Glenn Research Center ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/three-companies-to-help-nasa-advance-solar-array-technology-for-moon/)).

In 2021 NASA picked five companies for design studies: Astrobotic, Northrop Grumman's ATK Space Systems, Honeybee Robotics, Lockheed Martin and Maxar's Space Systems Loral. Each base contract was worth up to $700,000 ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/technology/nasa-industry-to-mature-vertical-solar-array-technologies-for-lunar-surface/)). In August 2022 NASA chose three of them to build prototypes for a total of $19.4 million: Astrobotic ($6.2M), Honeybee Robotics ($7M) and Lockheed Martin ($6.2M) ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/three-companies-to-help-nasa-advance-solar-array-technology-for-moon/)). Honeybee is now part of Blue Origin ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeybee_Robotics)).

The aim was to bring all three designs to Technology Readiness Level 6 through thermal-vacuum tests in Chamber A at Johnson Space Center ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)). In summer 2024 the Honeybee and Astrobotic prototypes were tested there ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/from-houston-to-the-moon-johnsons-thermal-vacuum-chamber-tests-lunar-solar-technology/)). NASA's stated goal is to deploy at least one system near the lunar south pole ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/from-houston-to-the-moon-johnsons-thermal-vacuum-chamber-tests-lunar-solar-technology/)). No flight unit has been ordered yet, as far as public sources show.

## Why Open Moon uses it

At the lunar poles the Sun stays low on the horizon. Hills and crater rims then cast long shadows over anything close to the ground. Raising the array on a mast lets it see over that terrain and collect more sunlight ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/three-companies-to-help-nasa-advance-solar-array-technology-for-moon/)). The Moon's spin axis sits at 88.5 degrees to the plane of its orbit, which is why some polar ridges get sunlight for long stretches ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_south_pole)).

In Open Moon, VSAT is the first source of power in milestone M04 (Power). Two 10 kW-class units go up on separate ridge points on small landers, before the larger [VSAT-XL](https://www.astrobotic.com/astrobotic-awarded-lunar-power-study-with-vsat-xl/) towers and a fission unit arrive. Astrobotic describes its own VSAT as a 10 kW system ([Astrobotic](https://www.astrobotic.com/astrobotic-awarded-lunar-power-study-with-vsat-xl/)). Without a relocatable tall array, the early base would depend on lander-mounted panels that lose power in shadow, or would have to wait for a reactor, which NASA has not yet contracted ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/glenn/nasa-seeks-industry-feedback-on-fission-surface-power/)).

## How it works

Each VSAT unit is a mast on a stable base, a solar blanket carried up the mast, and electronics to track the Sun, store power and send it out. NASA's goals were autonomous deployment on uneven ground, reliable retraction for moving, and low mass and packed volume ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)). Designs must also stay stable on slopes and resist abrasive dust ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/three-companies-to-help-nasa-advance-solar-array-technology-for-moon/)). The three prototypes are very different ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)):

| Team | Mast and array | Mobility and power |
|---|---|---|
| Astrobotic | Redwire Roll-Out Solar Array (ROSA) | Self-propelled cart; 3 kV AC power grid |
| Honeybee Robotics (LAMPS) | Helical metal-band mast, 12 in diameter; accordion-folded blankets of small silicon cells | 10 kW; designed for 10 years at the south pole |
| Lockheed Martin | Thin-wall rolled composite lenticular mast; flight-heritage blanket and deployer | Towed cart based on a GM lunar mobility vehicle |

ROSA is a flexible array that rolls out on booms. It was first tested on the International Space Station in 2017 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roll_Out_Solar_Array)). Astrobotic and Lockheed Martin planned to use a Kennedy Space Center electrodynamic dust shield ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)). The systems must survive the lunar night; NASA mentions up to 96 hours of darkness, and Chamber A can reach 20 K ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/from-houston-to-the-moon-johnsons-thermal-vacuum-chamber-tests-lunar-solar-technology/)). Mass per unit is not published.

## Limitations and open issues

- **Mass.** At preliminary design review, all selected designs were heavier than the project goals. NASA later judged the mass to be within the limits of a CLPS lander ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)).
- **Design risks.** NASA's 2023 risk list included Astrobotic's low-maturity high-voltage power system, Lockheed Martin mast retraction and buckling, Honeybee's heat rejection and solar cell maturity, and dust shield maturity ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)).
- **Test results not public.** NASA has said the two 2024 tests happened, but has not published whether TRL 6 was reached ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/from-houston-to-the-moon-johnsons-thermal-vacuum-chamber-tests-lunar-solar-technology/)). Lockheed Martin's test status is not reported in that article.
- **No flight mission yet.** In 2023 the project proposed a follow-on flight demonstration around 2028-2029 ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)). No such mission has been announced in the sources used here.
- **Night and shadow.** A 10 m mast does not remove the lunar night. The base still needs storage or [fission power](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220004670).
- **Cost risk.** All VSAT contracts were firm fixed price, and NASA flagged that cost growth could push vendors to cut technical work ([NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230012849)).
