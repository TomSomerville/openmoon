## Landing slope tolerance

**RESILIENCE: not published.** No primary or secondary source I found gives a maximum landing slope, in degrees, for the RESILIENCE lander.

Places searched:
- The [ispace spacecraft page](https://ispace-inc.com/spacecraft) and the [ispace-U.S. services page](https://ispace-us.com/services/). They list size, mass and payload only.
- The [12 Sept 2024 Mission 2 landing-zone press release (PDF)](https://d2xlcgjnpyakn4.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/ispace-Announces-Launch-Timing-RESILIENCE-Lander-Progress-and-Planned-Lunar-Landing-Zone-During-Mission-2-Update-Press-Conference.pdf). It says only that the Mare Frigoris site "meets the technical specifications of the RESILIENCE lander". It names sun-illumination duration and Earth visibility as criteria and gives no slope value.
- The [ispace-EUROPE LPSC 2025 abstract 1746](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2025/pdf/1746.pdf). It says the landing site selection team evaluated "terrain roughness, slope gradients, and surface hazards", but it gives no threshold.
- The [External Review Task Force report slides (42 pp.)](https://www.ispace-inc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/ssl4.eir-parts.netdoc9348tdnet278257800.pdf). They contain no slope figure.
- The [Wikipedia Hakuto-R Mission 2 article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakuto-R_Mission_2). It contains no slope figure.
- English and Japanese web searches ("slope", "tolerance", "傾斜", "許容") turned up nothing.

**Successors:**
- **APEX 1.0** (ispace-U.S.): the [APEX Payload User's Guide v01 (2025)](https://assets.ispace-us.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/28161539/APEX-PUG-v01.pdf) gives no slope tolerance. The related figures it does state:
  - "M2 DGNC includes hazard detection and avoidance capability for obstructions smaller than 1ft in diameter".
  - The landing legs have shock-absorbing elements that limit landing loads to below launch loads.
- **Series 3 / ULTRA:** not published. The [ispace Mission 3 page](https://ispace-inc.com/mission-3/) and the [ULTRA release](https://ispace-inc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/20260327-ULTRA.pdf) give no slope figure.

## Payload capacity

**RESILIENCE: up to 30 kg (design spec).**
- The [ispace spacecraft page](https://ispace-inc.com/spacecraft) and the [ispace Mission 2 page](https://ispace-inc.com/mission-2/) both state: "Payload Capacity: Up to 30kg. Summary: Lander designed to carry up to 30kg payload for use in Missions 1 and 2". The [ispace-U.S. services page](https://ispace-us.com/services/) gives the same text.
- The same pages give a mass of about 1,000 kg wet and about 340 kg dry, and a size of about 2.3 m high by 2.6 m wide with the legs extended.
- Conflict on size: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakuto-R_Mission_2) gives the width as 2.3 m, while ispace gives 2.6 m. The ispace figure is preferred.
- Demonstrated: none. The lander did not soft-land, so no payload was delivered intact.
- The payloads it carried included the TENACIOUS micro-rover, about 5 kg by [ispace](https://ispace-inc.com/mission-2/), plus the customer payloads listed in the [Sept 2024 release](https://d2xlcgjnpyakn4.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/ispace-Announces-Launch-Timing-RESILIENCE-Lander-Progress-and-Planned-Lunar-Landing-Zone-During-Mission-2-Update-Press-Conference.pdf). I found no published total for the payload mass actually flown.

## Landing record

- **Launch:** 15 January 2025 on a Falcon 9 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakuto-R_Mission_2); ispace's [Mission 2 page](https://ispace-inc.com/mission-2/) says January 2025).
- **Target site:** near the center of Mare Frigoris, at 60.5° N, 4.6° W, with backup sites also in Mare Frigoris ([ispace Sept 2024 release](https://d2xlcgjnpyakn4.cloudfront.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/ispace-Announces-Launch-Timing-RESILIENCE-Lander-Progress-and-Planned-Lunar-Landing-Zone-During-Mission-2-Update-Press-Conference.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakuto-R_Mission_2)).
- **Date:**
  - LROC gives "hard landing on 5 June 2025 18:13 UTC" ([LROC](https://www.lroc.im-ldi.com/images/1456)), and NASA gives "June 5, 2025, UTC" ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/lro/nasas-lro-views-ispace-hakuto-r-mission-2-moon-lander-impact-site)).
  - ispace gives 6 June 2025 in Japan time ([ispace](https://www.ispace-inc.com/2025/06/24/ispace-releases-technical-cause-analysis-for-hakuto-r-mission-2/)).
  - Wikipedia gives a planned touchdown of 19:17 UTC and says communications were lost about 90 s before then ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakuto-R_Mission_2)).
- **Impact site:** 60.4445° N, 355.4120° E, at −2431.6 m elevation. LRO imaged it on 11 June 2025 ([LROC](https://www.lroc.im-ldi.com/images/1456)).
- **Outcome:** hard landing (crash). The mission failed.
- **Published cause, per [ispace, 24 June 2025](https://www.ispace-inc.com/2025/06/24/ispace-releases-technical-cause-analysis-for-hakuto-r-mission-2/):**
  - In the final phase the lander's attitude was nearly vertical.
  - Valid measurements from the Laser Range Finder (LRF) arrived late, so the lander could not slow down enough to land.
  - The technical cause was "an anomaly in the LRF hardware". It was not the guidance software, propulsion or power.
  - ispace judged the likely root cause to be LRF performance that degraded in flight or was lower than expected. It found no installation-direction error.
- **External Review Task Force (27 March 2026):** ([press release](https://ispace-inc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/20260327-ERTF-Press-Release-.pdf), [report](https://www.ispace-inc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/ssl4.eir-parts.netdoc9348tdnet278257800.pdf))
  - The review used the CAST method and treated the failure as a systemic problem, not only a hardware one.
  - The report found that the lander estimated altitude using only its IMU (inertial measurement unit) and the LRF, and that the LRF was the only accurate altitude sensor.
  - It also found that the lander had about 4% extra propellant at the start of descent, which went unused.
  - Seven recommendations, including:
    - terrain-relative navigation (TRN)
    - using the spare propellant for a safer approach
    - better vendor selection
    - more testing
    - better fault detection, isolation and recovery (FDIR)
- **Bearing on the spec claims:** the landing never reached touchdown. Neither the 30 kg payload capacity nor any slope tolerance was tested on the surface.
