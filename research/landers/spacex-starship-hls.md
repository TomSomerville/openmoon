# SpaceX Starship HLS (crewed) and Starship cargo lander (Human-class Delivery Lander)

## Landing slope tolerance

**Short answer:** neither NASA nor SpaceX has published a maximum terrain slope that Starship HLS (crew or cargo) is designed to land on. Three related figures are published: a NASA landing-site slope requirement, a NASA tilt requirement, and an OIG description of the terrain. They measure different things and should not be merged.

1. **NASA landing-site requirement: surface slope < 10° (measured over 100 m).** This is listed under "Key Artemis III Landing Requirements" together with "Surface slope limit for EVA (2km): <20 deg" and "Land within 100 m of a target" ([NASA LaRC/ARC EDL Seminar 2024, "Artemis Human Landing System Update"](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240009596/downloads/ArtemisHLSUpdate_2024.pdf); repeated in [NASA "Artemis HLS Overview", Space Apps 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250009323/downloads/ArtemisOverview2025IntSPAPPSChallenge.pdf) and [NASA "Artemis HLS Deorbit, Descent, and Landing" (2022)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220011114/downloads/ArtemisHLSDDL_v3.pdf)). The slope maps in these slides show green for slopes < 10° and white contours for "regions of continuous 8 deg slope". This is a NASA mission and site-selection requirement for Artemis III. It is not the vehicle's design limit. It is not specific to SpaceX.
2. **NASA tilt requirement: 0–8° from local vertical (threshold), 0–5° (goal).** The requirement is HLS-R-0071 "Landing Site Vertical Orientation": "The HLS shall provide vertical orientation of 0 to 8 (threshold) and 0 to 5 (goal) from local vertical for surface operations." The rationale adds: "It is expected that the slope tolerance of the HLS will exceed the acceptable lander tilt angles for the safe and effective execution of critical crew functions" ([Chavers et al., "NASA's Human Landing System: The Strategy for the 2024 Mission and Future Sustainability", IEEE Aerospace 2020, NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001606/downloads/20200001606.pdf)). In other words, NASA expected the structural slope tolerance to be larger than 8°, but did not publish it. The March 2026 OIG report confirms that NASA set the tilt tolerance for both landers "as not to exceed 8 degrees to support all post-landing crew activities", for example operating the hatch ([NASA OIG IG-26-004, p. 24](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/final-report-ig-26-004-nasas-management-of-the-human-landing-system-contracts.pdf)). This requirement applies to the crew lander.
3. **OIG terrain context: south-pole slopes "of up to 20 degrees".** The OIG writes that "steep slopes of up to 20 degrees on the lunar South Pole present navigation and landing challenges". It also says that because Starship is 171 ft tall, "there is a risk that its momentum will continue after landing causing it to tip over" ([OIG IG-26-004, p. 24](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/final-report-ig-26-004-nasas-management-of-the-human-landing-system-contracts.pdf)). This describes the terrain. It is not a design capability. The OIG also says the uncrewed demo will not carry the elevator, so the elevator will not be tested "under possible surface tilt conditions" (same report, p. 28).
4. **Sustained-phase direction (no number).** NASA says later missions to the Artemis Base Camp need "more robust landing slope capabilities", but gives no figure ([NASA, "NASA's Artemis Human Landing Systems", IEEE 2022, NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210022893/downloads/HLS%20IEEE%20Paper_JPV_FINAL.pdf)). The contract SOW requires mockups that support "surface slope mitigation for crewed operations", again without a number ([HLS SOW, contract 80MSFC20C0034](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/80MSFC20C0034-P00010_Att_J-01_SOW_RIF_TAGGED.pdf)).
5. **Cargo variant (HDL):** the [HLS-IRD-010 Rev C HDL-to-cargo IRD (Apr 2026)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007769/downloads/HLS-IRD-010_HDL%20System%20to%20Cargo_Revision_C.pdf) contains no slope or tilt requirement. It says "NASA is responsible for determining landing zone for each mission". The HDL System Requirements Document it refers to was not found publicly.

**Searches with no vehicle-specific slope figure:** NTRS for HLS-RQMT-001 and HLS-RQMT-002 (the Contractor System Requirements Document named in the SOW, which was not found publicly), NASA OIG IG-26-004, the HLS contract SOW, NASA HLS pages, the SpaceX website (its pages are rendered by JavaScript and returned no text when fetched), and [Wikipedia: Starship HLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_HLS), which gives no slope figure.

## Payload capacity

**Cargo variant (HDL)**
- **NASA interface requirement: 15 t.** "[HDL-CP-033] Cargo Mass: The Cargo shall not exceed 15 t, inclusive of any cargo-unique adapters." HDL-CP-056 says this "bounds the design for the 15 t (33069.3 lbm) mass capability." Related limits in the same document: a cargo envelope of either 6.35 m diameter × 8.8 m high or 4.57 m diameter × 7.8 m high; a cargo centre of mass 0–4.5 m above the interface plane; a requirement that the HDL stay stable with up to 22 t of attached cargo after landing (HDL-CP-037); and an Earth-to-touchdown transit time of no more than 210 days ([HLS-IRD-010 Rev C, 29 Apr 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260007769/downloads/HLS-IRD-010_HDL%20System%20to%20Cargo_Revision_C.pdf)). This IRD applies to any HDL provider, so it covers Blue Origin as well as SpaceX. The document is posted on NTRS but carries the footer "not approved for public release".
- **NASA public figures (both providers): about 12–15 t.** NASA gives "approximately 26,000 – 33,000 pounds (12 to 15 metric tons)" and says service begins "no earlier than the Artemis VII mission" ([NASA, 19 Apr 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/artemis-campaign-development-division/human-landing-system-program/work-underway-on-large-cargo-landers-for-nasas-artemis-moon-missions/); same figure in [NASA IAC 2025, Creech](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250008728/downloads/25%2008%2026%20IAC_Creech%20BP-1.pdf)). The NASA Moon to Mars white paper lists the HDL at "0 – 12,000 or 15,000" kg ([NASA ACR24 "Lunar Surface Cargo"](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/acr24-lunar-surface-cargo.pdf)). By Oct 2026 NASA gives a single figure, "approximately 33,000 pounds (15 mT)" ([NASA IAC 2026, Creech](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260009199/downloads/HLS%20IAC%202026%20Presentation%20FINAL.pdf)). NASA does not say which provider sits at 12 t and which at 15 t.
- **SpaceX claim: 100 t.** "Starship can land 100 tons on the lunar surface," according to Aarti Matthews, SpaceX Starship HLS program manager, quoted in May 2022 ([Ars Technica, 2 May 2022](https://arstechnica.com/science/2022/05/spacex-engineer-says-nasa-should-plan-for-starships-significant-capability/)). Wikipedia's 100,000 kg infobox figure cites this same article ([Wikipedia: Starship HLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_HLS)). I found no SpaceX document that states this figure.
- **Conflict:** the NASA requirement and NASA public figure (15 t, or 12–15 t) differ from the SpaceX claim (100 t) by about 6–8 times. Neither figure has been demonstrated.

**Crew HLS**
- **NASA requirements for the original HLS baseline:** HLS-R-0318 "deliver 865 kg (threshold) and 965 kg (goal) from Lunar Orbit to the lunar surface", based on two crew plus government equipment. HLS-R-0356 requires 100 kg of science payload. HLS-R-0318a (sustained phase) requires "at least 1,595 kg from the Gateway to the lunar surface" ([NASA HLS Strategy 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001606/downloads/20200001606.pdf)). These are minimum requirements from 2020. They are not Starship's capability.
- **Starship crew-variant capacity: not published** by NASA. The only SpaceX figure is the general "100 tons" claim quoted above.

## Landing record

- **Lunar landings or attempts: none** for either variant as of 18 Nov 2026. No Starship has flown beyond Earth orbit ([Wikipedia: Starship HLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_HLS)).
- **Earth flight tests (Starship/Super Heavy, not HLS-configured):** Flights 1–11 ran from 20 Apr 2023 to 13 Oct 2025. On Flight 3 (14 Mar 2024), propellant was transferred between two tanks inside one ship. Flight 4 made the first controlled landings of the ship and booster on water. Flight 5 made the first booster catch. Flights 7, 8 and 9 each lost the ship ([OIG IG-26-004, pp. 6–7, 13](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/final-report-ig-26-004-nasas-management-of-the-human-landing-system-contracts.pdf); [NASA, Flight 3](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/artemis-campaign-development-division/human-landing-system-program/nasa-artemis-mission-progresses-with-spacex-starship-test-flight)).
  - Flight 14 (28 Sep 2026, Block 3): reached orbit for the first time despite losing engines, then splashed down in the Pacific ([Wikipedia: Starship flight 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_14)).
  - Flight 15 (13–14 Nov 2026): about 22.5 h in orbit, then the tower caught the ship, the first recovery of a Starship upper stage from orbit. The booster's catch was aborted and it was lost in the Gulf ([Wikipedia: Starship flight 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_15)).
- **Ground and HLS-specific tests:** NASA shows a docking-system qualification and crew-in-the-loop testing. It also lists "Cabin One" crew-cabin build and tests and a landing-leg demo as 2026–27 milestones ([NASA IAC 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260009199/downloads/HLS%20IAC%202026%20Presentation%20FINAL.pdf)).

## Status (Nov 2026)

- **Ship-to-ship cryogenic propellant transfer:** not yet flown. It was planned for March 2025 and then moved to March 2026 ([OIG IG-26-004](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/final-report-ig-26-004-nasas-management-of-the-human-landing-system-contracts.pdf)). NASA's Oct 2026 chart places the "Prop Transfer Demo" in Q1 2027 ([NASA IAC 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260009199/downloads/HLS%20IAC%202026%20Presentation%20FINAL.pdf)). Wikipedia lists it as not yet scheduled as of Sep 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_Propellant_Transfer_Demonstration)).
- **Uncrewed lunar landing demo (crew HLS):** the OIG said in Mar 2026 that it was "expected to slip to the end of 2026" ([OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/final-report-ig-26-004-nasas-management-of-the-human-landing-system-contracts.pdf)). NASA's Oct 2026 chart shows the first tanker flight in Q4 2027 and the uncrewed demo mission around Q1 2028 ([NASA IAC 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260009199/downloads/HLS%20IAC%202026%20Presentation%20FINAL.pdf)). The two sources conflict, and the NASA chart is the more recent.
- **Artemis III (2027):** a crewed Earth-orbit mission that tests rendezvous and docking with lander test articles from SpaceX and Blue Origin. **Artemis IV (2028):** the first crewed landing. Both providers are "targeting to be able to support a crewed lunar landing in 2028" ([NASA, 4 Nov 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/nasa-shares-plans-to-accelerate-artemis-return-to-moon/)). NASA has said "lander readiness will determine which provider" flies the crew ([NASA, 3 Mar 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/nasa-strengthens-artemis-adds-mission-refines-overall-architecture/)). The Oct 2026 IAC deck gives Artemis IV as "early 2028" with the lander "TBD" ([NASA IAC 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260009199/downloads/HLS%20IAC%202026%20Presentation%20FINAL.pdf)). So Starship HLS is not confirmed as the Artemis IV lander.
- **Cargo (HDL):** NASA says no earlier than Artemis VII ([NASA, Apr 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/artemis-campaign-development-division/human-landing-system-program/work-underway-on-large-cargo-landers-for-nasas-artemis-moon-missions/)). NASA intends to award SpaceX the delivery of the JAXA pressurized rover, NET FY2032 ([NASA IAC 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260009199/downloads/HLS%20IAC%202026%20Presentation%20FINAL.pdf)).
